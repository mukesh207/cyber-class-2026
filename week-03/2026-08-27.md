# Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES) & Events vs Incidents

> **Course:** Cybersecurity
> **Date:** 2026-08-27 | **Session:** 07

---

## 1. What is it?
The **Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES)** is a comprehensive framework that outlines the lifecycle of a penetration test, ensuring consistency and quality. It guides a pentester from initial client meetings (pre-engagement) to the final report.

An **Event** is any observable occurrence in a system (e.g., a user logging in), recorded as a **Log**. An **Incident** is a specific security event that negatively affects the confidentiality, integrity, or availability (CIA triad) of information assets.

## 2. Why does it happen?
**PTES** exists to provide a standardized, legal, and thorough approach to security testing. Without it, pentesting would lack structure, leading to incomplete testing, undefined boundaries, or legal liabilities.
**Incidents** happen due to vulnerabilities in systems, misconfigurations, or successful exploits by attackers.

## 3. How does it work?
The PTES framework works in seven distinct phases:

```text
[Pre-engagement] --> [Intelligence Gathering] --> [Threat Modeling] --> [Vulnerability Analysis]
                                                                                      |
                                                                                      v
                 [Reporting] <------------- [Post-Exploitation] <---------- [Exploitation]
```

1. **Pre-engagement Interactions**: Defining the scope (In-Scope vs. Out-of-Scope) and signing an **NDA (Non-Disclosure Agreement)**.
2. **Intelligence Gathering (Reconnaissance)**: Collecting data on the target. Can be **Passive** (OSINT, public records) or **Active** (directly interacting, like Ping).
3. **Threat Modeling**: Prioritizing attack vectors based on target business logic.
4. **Vulnerability Analysis**: Finding flaws using **Automated** tools (scanners) or **Manual** inspection.
5. **Exploitation**: The actual "hacking" phase to gain unauthorized access.
6. **Post-Exploitation**: Maintaining access, privilege escalation, and pivoting.
7. **Reporting**: Documenting findings with **CVE** (vulnerability ID), **CVSS** (severity score), and actionable **Remediation**.

## 4. Where is it used / found?
- **PTES** is used by cybersecurity consultancy firms, red teams, and freelance pentesters during authorized engagements.
- **Events and Logs** are generated continuously by every IT asset (servers, firewalls, applications, endpoints).
- **Incidents** are identified, investigated, and handled by a Security Operations Center (SOC) or an Incident Response (IR) team.

## 5. How do I identify it?
- A **professional pentest** is identified by strict adherence to scoping, authorized IP addresses, and detailed methodology and reporting.
- **Passive Reconnaissance** is theoretically impossible to identify from the target's perspective, as the attacker never interacts with the target's systems.
- **Active Reconnaissance** (like port scans) can be identified in firewall or IDS logs as unexpected or high-volume traffic from a single IP address.

## 6. Hands-on / Lab Methodology
*Context: Conducting a penetration test in an authorized lab based on PTES.*
- **What to observe first:** The scoping document and NDA to know exactly what systems are allowed.
- **Attack surface:** Identified during the Intelligence Gathering phase (IPs, subdomains, open ports).
- **Initial hypothesis:** Based on Threat Modeling, predicting which vulnerabilities are most likely to exist and succeed.
- **What to test:** Vulnerability Analysis (e.g., running Nessus or manually testing web inputs).
- **How to test it safely (authorized lab):** Only target in-scope IP addresses and use rate-limiting to avoid crashing services.
- **Expected result:** Identifying a valid vulnerability and exploiting it to demonstrate the business impact.
- **How to interpret the result:** Document the findings and calculate the CVSS score to gauge the severity of the vulnerability.

## 7. Failed Attempts & Why
*Context: During the exploitation phase.*
- **What was tried:** Running an automated exploit script against an out-of-date web server.
- **Why it failed:** The server had a Web Application Firewall (WAF) blocking the automated payload, or the scanner reported a false positive.
- **What that tells us:** Automated analysis has limitations. The vulnerability might not exist, or the exploit needs to be manually obfuscated.
- **What to try next:** Manual Vulnerability Analysis to understand the application logic, or bypassing the WAF with custom payloads.

## 8. Example
A company hires a pentester.
1. They sign an **NDA** and agree that only `app.target.com` is **In-Scope**.
2. The pentester uses **Passive Recon** (WHOIS) and **Active Recon** (Nmap port scan).
3. **Automated Vulnerability Analysis** flags an old Apache version.
4. The pentester **Exploits** it to get a reverse shell.
5. In **Post-Exploitation**, they read the `/etc/shadow` file to prove they gained root access.
6. They deliver a **Report** detailing the **CVE**, a **CVSS** of 9.8, and specific **Remediation** steps.

## 9. Impact
If a penetration test is not properly scoped or executed (ignoring PTES), it can cause unplanned downtime (crashing production servers) or legal consequences for the pentester.
If a security **Incident** is not prevented or detected by a SOC, it can lead to data breaches, financial loss, and severe reputation damage for the organization.

## 10. Detection
- **Detecting Active Reconnaissance:** Blue teams (SOC analysts) can detect active recon by monitoring firewall logs and Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) for anomalous ICMP requests (ping sweeps) or rapid port scanning (e.g., Nmap).
- **Detecting Exploitation:** Identifying unusual child processes spawning from web server users, or unexpected outbound connections (reverse shells) in network logs.

## 11. Prevention / Remediation
- **Preventing Scope Creep/Legal Issues:** Always have a signed NDA and a strictly defined Scope of Work before sending a single packet.
- **Remediating Vulnerabilities:** The pentest report should provide specific instructions, such as applying a particular patch ("Update Apache to version 2.4.50"), changing default configurations, or modifying code to sanitize inputs.

## 12. Related Concepts
- **CIA Triad** -> **Security Incident**: An incident is defined by the loss or compromise of Confidentiality, Integrity, or Availability.
- **Vulnerability Scanning** -> **Manual Analysis**: Scanners provide the baseline coverage, while manual analysis validates findings and deepens the assessment to find logic flaws.
- **Blue Team (Defenders)** -> **Logs & Events**: The defense relies entirely on collecting and correlating logs to detect the pentester's or attacker's actions.

## 13. Important Commands / Syntax
- `ping <target_IP>`: Sends an ICMP Echo Request. A form of **Active Reconnaissance** because the packet physically touches the target network, leaving a log entry in their systems.

## 14. Interview Questions
**Q: What is the difference between an Event and an Incident?**
> An event is any observable occurrence on a network (like a login attempt) recorded in a log. An incident is a specific event that causes harm or violates a security policy (like a successful data breach).

**Q: Why is ping considered Active Reconnaissance?**
> Because sending an ICMP Echo Request interacts directly with the target's infrastructure. This interaction is logged by their firewall or IDS, meaning the target can detect your activity.

**Q: Why is an NDA and strict Scoping legally critical before a pentest?**
> Unauthorized hacking is illegal. The scoping document provides explicit legal authorization for what can be attacked, while the NDA protects the client's sensitive data and the pentester from legal liability.

**Q: What is the difference between Manual and Automated Vulnerability Analysis?**
> Automated analysis uses tools to quickly scan for known vulnerabilities (CVEs) but can yield false positives and miss business logic flaws. Manual analysis uses human expertise to find complex, chained, or zero-day vulnerabilities that scanners miss.

## 16. Key Takeaways
- **PTES** is the gold standard lifecycle for conducting a professional penetration test.
- Never test without a signed **NDA** and a clearly defined **Scope**.
- **Passive recon** doesn't touch the target; **Active recon** interacts directly and can be logged.
- **Automated scanning** is broad but shallow; **Manual analysis** is deep and contextual.
- A **Log** records an **Event**; an **Incident** is when that event causes harm.
- The **Reporting** phase is the most critical deliverable for the client, translating technical findings into business risk and actionable remediation.

## 17. One-Minute Revision
A penetration test follows the PTES framework: pre-engagement (scoping & NDA), intelligence gathering (passive/active recon), threat modeling, vulnerability analysis (automated/manual), exploitation, post-exploitation, and reporting. Without scoping and an NDA, pentesting is illegal. Active recon (like ping) touches the target and generates logs/events; if malicious, these events become security incidents. The final report must contain clear vulnerability IDs (CVE), severity scores (CVSS), and actionable remediation steps.
